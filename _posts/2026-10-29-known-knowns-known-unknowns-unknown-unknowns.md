## From robot demonstrations to risk-aware manufacturing

On October 28, 2026, I joined an Arizona State University discussion with Nationwide associates to talk about robotics and autonomous systems, including humanoid and quadruped robot demonstrations. The conversation focused on a practical question: how can companies understand and manage the risks that arise when intelligent machines operate in real manufacturing environments?

![AI and robotics seminar during the ASU–Nationwide discussion](/assets/04_BlogPosts/06_ASU_Nationwide/01_Seminar.jpeg)
*Discussing AI, robotics, and autonomous-system risk with Nationwide associates.*

The discussion brought to mind a useful way to organize uncertainty across four categories:

- **Known knowns:** what we can measure and model, such as robot position, speed, torque, production-cycle time, sensor status, and task requirements.
- **Known unknowns:** what we recognize as uncertain, such as changing payloads, sensor noise, tool wear, communication delays, model error, and variations in human behavior.
- **Unknown knowns:** information that exists somewhere in the organization or system but is not visible to the current decision process. Examples include undocumented operator knowledge, maintenance history stored in a separate system, unused sensor data, or a safety concern known by one team but not communicated to another.
- **Unknown unknowns:** failures or interactions that have not yet been observed, such as an unusual combination of equipment faults, environmental conditions, software behavior, and human intervention.

The objective of a modern manufacturing system is not to claim that every unknown can be eliminated. It is to make unknown knowns discoverable, convert them into documented knowledge, and build enough visibility, testing capability, and operational evidence that uncertainty can be identified early, bounded, and managed responsibly.

## Connecting artificial intelligence, robotics, and digital twins

Artificial intelligence provides the ability to detect patterns in data, classify operating conditions, identify anomalies, and estimate the state of a process. Robotics provides the physical capability to act in the world. A digital twin connects these capabilities by maintaining a computational representation of the robot, workcell, process, and surrounding environment.

Together, these technologies create a feedback loop:

1. Sensors and controllers collect information from the physical workcell.
2. AI models analyze the data and identify normal and abnormal behavior.
3. The digital twin evaluates possible actions and operating conditions before changes are applied to the physical system.
4. The robot executes within defined safety and operational limits.
5. The results are recorded as evidence for future analysis, validation, and improvement.

This loop helps manufacturing teams move from reacting to failures toward anticipating them. A digital twin can support what-if analysis, virtual commissioning, safety-case development, predictive maintenance, and operator training. AI can help identify patterns that are difficult to detect manually, while the robot remains constrained by explicit task, workspace, and safety rules.

## Why this matters to manufacturing and insurance

For manufacturers, the value is not only higher automation. It is better understanding of how a system behaves across its operating envelope. Before deploying an autonomous workcell, a company can study collision scenarios, degraded sensors, communication loss, unusual loads, human proximity, and recovery procedures in a controlled environment.

For insurers and business leaders, this evidence can support a more precise view of operational risk. Useful evidence may include validated safety procedures, robot and sensor histories, digital-twin test results, maintenance records, access controls, incident logs, and documented human-override mechanisms. These records do not make a system risk-free, but they make the system's risks more visible and its controls more assessable.

The central principle is simple: trustworthy autonomy requires more than an intelligent model. It requires traceable data, clear operating boundaries, testing across expected and unexpected conditions, safe human interaction, and a documented response when the system encounters something outside its experience.

## Building manufacturing systems that can explain themselves

The next generation of manufacturing will combine physical automation with models that can explain what the system observed, what it predicted, and why it took a particular action. Digital twins provide a structured environment for testing those decisions. AI provides tools for learning from operational data. Robotics turns decisions into physical activity.

When these technologies are integrated carefully, companies can make unknowns more manageable. They can recover unknown knowns from disconnected data and operator experience, identify known risks, expose hidden assumptions, simulate rare conditions, and create an evidence trail for engineering, operations, safety, and insurance decisions.

The ASU–Nationwide discussion reinforced that robotics research must address both technical performance and the broader context in which autonomous systems are adopted. Understanding robots is only one part of the challenge. The larger goal is to build manufacturing systems that are capable, measurable, explainable, and safe enough to earn the confidence of the people and organizations that rely on them.
